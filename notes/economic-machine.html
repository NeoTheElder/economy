    Section 1 · 0:00 – 1:13

    The map: three forces

    Three forces move every economy. Each force works on a different time scale.

    Dalio says the economy works like a simple machine. It has a few simple parts. It has a very large number of simple transactions that repeat again and again. Human nature drives these transactions.

    The transactions create three forces:

    1. Productivity growth. The slow, steady trend line.
    2. The short-term debt cycle. A wave of about 5 to 8 years.
    3. The long-term debt cycle. A wave of about 75 to 100 years.

    Put the three forces on top of each other. The result is a template. The template shows where the economy was, where it is now, and where it probably goes next.

    Section 2 · 1:13 – 3:29

    Transactions: the building block

    An economy is the sum of its transactions. Total spending drives the economy.

    In each transaction, a buyer gives money or credit to a seller. The seller gives goods, services, or financial assets in return.

    • Credit spends like money. So total spending = money + credit.
    • Divide total spending by the quantity sold. The result is the price: price = spending ÷ quantity.
    • A market is all the buyers and all the sellers of one thing: wheat, cars, stocks.
    • An economy is all the transactions in all its markets.

    The biggest buyer and seller: the government

    The government has two parts. Keep them separate. The difference becomes very important later.

    • The central government collects taxes and spends money.
    • The central bank controls the amount of money and credit. It sets interest rates and it prints new money.

    Section 3 · 3:29 – 6:21

    Credit: the biggest and most volatile part

    Credit appears from a promise. One person's spending is another person's income. Together these two facts make growth feed on itself.

    Lenders want to make their money into more money. Borrowers want to buy something now that they cannot pay for now, such as a house, a car, or a new business.

    • The borrower promises to repay the principal plus interest.
    • High interest rates mean less borrowing. Low interest rates mean more borrowing.
    • When a borrower promises and a lender believes, credit exists. Any two people can create it.
    • At once, credit becomes debt. The debt is an asset to the lender and a liability to the borrower.
    • When the borrower repays, the asset and the liability disappear.

    Why credit matters so much

    Credit lets a borrower spend more. Every dollar you spend, someone else earns. So more spending means more income for someone else. Lenders lend more to people with more income. That person spends more, and the pattern continues.

    A lender checks two things before lending. Together they make a borrower creditworthy.

    Principal:
    the amount borrowed.
    Interest:
    the extra amount the borrower pays for the loan.
    Collateral:
    assets the lender can sell if the borrower cannot repay.
    Creditworthy:
    able to repay (income large compared with debt) and has collateral.

    Section 4 · 6:21 – 10:20

    Why borrowing makes cycles

    Productivity matters most in the long run. Credit matters most in the short run.

    People who invent and work hard raise their productivity. Over time, productivity raises living standards. But productivity grows slowly and steadily. It does not cause the big swings.

    Debt causes the swings. When you borrow, you can consume more than you produce. When you repay, you must consume less than you produce.

    Borrowing is borrowing from your future self

    To buy something you cannot pay for now, you spend more than you earn. Later you must spend less than you earn to repay. That is a cycle. Every time you borrow, you create a cycle. This is true for one person and for a whole economy.

    Imagine an economy with no credit. Then the only way to spend more is to earn more, and the only way to earn more is to produce more. Growth follows the productivity line, with no debt cycles.

    Money and credit are different

    Money settles a transaction. Pay cash for a beer and the deal ends at once. Put the beer on a bar tab and you create credit. The bartender holds an asset and you hold a liability until you pay.

    Most of what people call money is credit. In the US at the time of the video, credit was about $50 trillion and money was about $3 trillion.

    Section 5 · 10:20 – 11:56

    Good credit, bad credit, and growth

    Credit is good when it produces the income to repay it. Credit is bad when it finances consumption that cannot be repaid.

    With credit, an economy spends more. Incomes can rise faster than productivity in the short run. They cannot do this in the long run.

    Follow the transactions

    You earn $100,000 and have no debt. You borrow $10,000 and spend $110,000. Your spending is the next person's income. That person borrows 10% of $110,000 and spends $121,000. The pattern feeds on itself.

    But borrowing creates cycles. If the cycle goes up, it must come down at some point.

    Section 6 · 11:56 – 14:16

    The short-term debt cycle

    The central bank steers this cycle with interest rates. It lasts about 5 to 8 years.

    1. Expansion. Credit is easy to get. Spending rises.
    2. Inflation. Spending and incomes grow faster than the production of goods. Prices rise.
    3. The central bank raises interest rates. Fewer people can afford new loans. Payments on existing debt rise.
    4. Spending slows. Because one person's spending is another person's income, incomes fall.
    5. Recession. Prices fall. Economists call falling prices deflation.
    6. The central bank lowers interest rates when the recession is too severe and inflation is no longer a problem. Borrowing and spending increase. A new expansion starts.

    In this cycle, the only limit on spending is the willingness of lenders to lend and borrowers to borrow. Easy credit means expansion. Hard credit means recession.

    Each cycle ends a little higher, with more debt

    People prefer to borrow and spend more rather than repay debt. This is human nature. So each top and each bottom of the short cycle has more growth and more debt than the one before.

    Inflation:
    prices rise in general.
    Deflation:
    prices fall in general.
    Recession:
    economic activity decreases. In this cycle, lower interest rates can end it.

    Section 7 · 14:16 – 16:48

    The long-term debt cycle

    Over decades, debts rise faster than incomes. Rising incomes and asset prices hide the risk until the debt burden is too big.

    People become more indebted, but lenders lend even more freely. Everyone looks at what happened recently. Incomes rise. Asset prices rise. The stock market rises. It is a boom. Buying goods and assets with borrowed money pays. When many people do this, the result is a bubble.

    Dalio calls the ratio of debt to income the debt burden: debt burden = debt ÷ income. While incomes keep rising, the burden stays manageable. Rising asset prices also keep borrowers creditworthy for a long time.

    The long-term debt peak

    Over decades, debt payments grow. At some point they grow faster than incomes. People must reduce spending. Incomes fall. People become less creditworthy, so borrowing falls. Debt payments still rise, so spending drops further. The cycle reverses.

    • United States and Europe: 2008
    • Japan: 1989
    • United States: 1929
    Debt burden:
    debt divided by income.
    Bubble:
    many people buy assets with borrowed money because prices keep rising.

    Section 8 · 16:48 – 19:09

    Deleveraging: the cycle in reverse

    A deleveraging looks like a recession. But interest rates are already near 0%, so cutting them cannot help.

    In a deleveraging, people reduce spending. Incomes fall. Credit disappears. Asset prices drop. Banks are under pressure. The stock market crashes. Social tension rises. Each event makes the next one worse.

    Borrowers cannot borrow enough to make their debt payments. They must sell assets. The rush to sell pushes prices down. Collateral loses value, so borrowers become even less creditworthy. People feel poor.

    RecessionDeleveraging
    Which cycleShort-termLong-term
    Interest ratesRoom to cutAlready near 0%
    Does a rate cut work?Yes. Borrowing restarts.No. Rates cannot go lower.
    Debt burdenManageableToo big to repay in full
    Lenders and borrowersLend and borrow again after a cutLenders stop lending. Borrowers stop borrowing.
    Examples in the videoThe normal 5 to 8 year dipUS 1930s, US 2008

    Think of the whole economy as one person who is no longer creditworthy. Lenders see that the debts are too large to repay in full. Borrowers feel crippled by debt and do not want more.

    Deleveraging:
    the period when debt burdens must come down after a long-term debt peak.

    Section 9 · 19:09 – 26:14

    Four ways to reduce the debt burden

    Three ways push prices down. One way pushes prices up. Every modern deleveraging used all four.

    1. Cut spending (austerity)

    People, businesses, banks, and governments reduce spending to repay debt. You expect the debt burden to fall. It rises. Spending cuts lower other people's incomes. Incomes fall faster than debts are repaid. Businesses cut costs, so unemployment rises. This way is deflationary and painful.

    2. Reduce debt (defaults and restructuring)

    Many borrowers cannot repay. A borrower's debt is a lender's asset. People fear that banks cannot repay them, so they withdraw their money. Banks lose money. People, businesses, and banks default. This severe contraction is a depression. People find that much of their wealth was never really there.

    Many lenders prefer to restructure. They accept less money, or a longer time, or a lower interest rate. A little of something is better than all of nothing. But income and asset values fall faster than debt does. This way is also deflationary and painful.

    3. Redistribute wealth

    The government collects less tax because incomes and jobs fall. It must spend more on the unemployed and on stimulus. Its deficit grows. To pay, it raises taxes on the wealthy, who hold most of the wealth. The “have-nots” resent the “haves”. The “haves” resent the “have-nots”. Tension can rise inside countries and between debtor and creditor countries. In the 1930s, this led to extreme political change, including Hitler's rise to power.

    4. Print money

    Most of what people thought was money was credit. When credit disappears, people do not have enough money. With rates near 0%, the central bank must print money. Unlike the first three ways, printing is inflationary and stimulative. The US Federal Reserve printed over $2 trillion after 2008.

    Why the central bank and the government must work together

    The central bank can print money. But it can buy only financial assets. That helps only people who own financial assets. The central government can buy goods and services and give money to people. But it cannot print money. So the two must cooperate.

    Austerity:
    cutting spending to repay debt.
    Default:
    a borrower does not repay.
    Restructuring:
    lender and borrower change the contract so the debt shrinks.
    Depression:
    a severe economic contraction with widespread defaults.

    Section 10 · 26:14 – 29:37

    The “beautiful” deleveraging

    Balance the deflationary ways with the inflationary way. Get income growth above the interest rate on the debt.

    A deleveraging can be ugly or it can be beautiful. In a beautiful deleveraging:

    • debts fall compared with income,
    • real economic growth is positive,
    • inflation is not a problem.

    Does printing money cause inflation? Not if it replaces credit that disappeared. Spending is what matters. A dollar of spending paid with money has the same effect on price as a dollar paid with credit.

    The key test: income growth against the interest rate

    Dalio's example: debt equals one year of national income (100%). The interest rate on the debt is 2%. Debt grows 2% a year from interest alone. If income grows only 1%, the burden never falls. The central bank must print enough to push income growth above the interest rate.

    This model is simple. Debt grows only by interest. Income grows at a constant rate. This matches the example in the video.

    Printing is easy to abuse because people prefer it to the other ways. Too much printing causes very high inflation, as in Germany in the 1920s.

    Recovery: reflation

    Reflation:
    the phase after a deleveraging when lending, spending, and growth return.

    Watch 26:14

    Section 11 · 29:37 – 31:00

    Three rules of thumb

    Keep debt growth below income growth, and income growth below productivity growth. Then raise productivity.

    1. Do not let debt rise faster than income. Debt burdens will crush you at some point.
    2. Do not let income rise faster than productivity. You will become uncompetitive.
    3. Do all you can to raise productivity. In the long run, it matters most.

    The template is simple: put the short cycle on the long cycle, and put both on the productivity line. Dalio says this applies to one person and to policy makers.
